Technology

The only thing that gave me a little sense of accomplishment this year was building my own tech blog, where I can record my technical growth and the bits and pieces of life.

Since I set up the blog at the beginning of the year, I’ve published 45 posts so far.

As my About page says, I don’t set too high a bar for its quality — I just try to record technical and life lessons that are easy to forget. Even though readers may not pay much attention, I myself treasure them like a collection of prized possessions, because after all, this is a fortune. When I look back at this year-end summary a few years from now, I hope I can say I didn’t regret wasting my years, nor feel ashamed of doing nothing.

I’m a 90s kid who doesn’t enjoy playing video games. Only by checking the stats dashboard every day do I get back a bit of motivation to work.

This year’s technical work was mostly centered on multimedia — every day I wrestled with audio and video. In the first half of the year I was developing a TCP-based chat app called 百度Hi for mac. Of course, to that CEO — obsessed with AI, bent on self-driving cars, whose products never landed well, and who could never retain core engineers — I handed in my resignation. In the second half of the year I devoted myself to short-video multimedia development: Open GL, GPUImage, AV Foundation…… grinding away (kengchi!). As of writing this post, I’ve just finished the last page of AV Foundation. After working on technology for so long, I’ve come to feel deeply that some fields need persistent cultivation — you can’t blindly follow trends; you have to stay grounded.

28 years old, a 90s kid. This year I felt no real sense of achievement in technology. Instead, I have this reflection:

Maybe the greats are all destined, and unfortunately, I wasn’t.

So my advice for house hunting: watch your mouth, bring enough water, move your legs — and don’t eat too much.

Harbin Hukou and Beijing Housing Fund

I’m a coder drifting in Beijing — I went straight to Beijing after graduating from university. My hukou isn’t registered in Harbin. Based on what I learned from asking around after visiting most of the sales offices, here’s how the housing fund works:

  1. To use Beijing’s housing fund in Harbin, you must have a Harbin hukou.
  2. To get a Harbin hukou, you must buy a house, or have a collective hukou through employer acceptance, etc.
  3. To buy a house in Harbin with Beijing's housing fund, you must have a Harbin hukou — nowhere else will accept it.

Writing this, I really want to curse, but to maintain the demeanor of a Confucian scholar, I held back. I have to say, this is a chicken-or-egg problem.

If rural hukou holders want to become Harbin citizens because each of us rural folk is allotted a piece of land, then I’d ask the government to take back my land and change my hukou to Harbin. If that lousy plot could make me rich, why would I have left home to drift in Beijing?

Going home for the New Year, my mind wasn’t really on the holiday — my mood was conflicted. After the seventh day of the new year, I went back to work in Beijing. My hometown is Hailun, Suihua.

Right Time Right Place Right People

Buying a house is absolutely a matter of the right time, the right place, and the right people.

That week while my parents looked at houses for me, I basically had no mood to work either. My boss (manager) could see how I was feeling — I could tell. In the end, he gave me a week off to go home and buy the house.

Because that week I was preparing income certificates for various banks, printing bank statements for loan templates from various Harbin banks, housing fund documents, etc.

To take Beijing’s housing fund out for a down payment, I had to withdraw from the Beijing housing fund system. Which essentially means leaving Beijing. In plain terms, I had to quit my job.

This experience is unforgettable to this day. At the time, I was truly stuck between a rock and a hard place.

The Loan

After going home and handling everything, the most infuriating thing happened.

I paid a down payment of 610k+ and took a loan of 600k. Harbin Bank is the most [bleep] bank I’ve ever seen. My bank statements already exceeded two months of the monthly payment, but they still demanded that my girlfriend’s statements meet the same standard as mine. I was grinding my teeth at Harbin Bank in disgust. In the end, I got a commercial loan from China Merchants Bank to buy the house. At this point, I want to say: if you can meet this bank’s standards, you’re not far from the middle class — you’re already well-off among the well-off.

Here I’ll mention a few traps:

  1. Bank statements for the loan must be printed on site. If your statements are fake, don’t go for the loan — you’ll gain nothing and lose everything.
  2. If your repayment ability isn’t enough, you need a co-signer. They’re required to prepare the same materials as you. This basically means relying on relatives. Unfortunately, the relative in my family who could do it already had a loan.
  3. When taking out a loan, the loan officer always recommends various wealth-management products. Don’t buy them, trust me.
  4. The bank account for the loan must have a deposit covering at least three months of repayment.

I got screwed by rule 4. After paying the down payment, saying I was penniless would be an exaggeration, but I had at most 2,000+ yuan. After covering the travel costs for two people back to Beijing, we could barely afford food and lodging. And they still wanted me to deposit 30,000? Isn’t that ridiculous? In the end, I borrowed some money from a good friend and my sister and deposited it. Sigh, what absolute nonsense.

I’ve gone back to pre-liberation days. What more do they want from me?

By this point, I think a country where even the most basic housing for ordinary people is a problem is no longer a good country.

Note: After the bank disburses the loan, be sure to go to the sales office and ask for the invoices. Only the down-payment invoice + the balance invoice = the property title. If either of the two invoices is missing, you may not get the title deed. The annoying part is that the salespeople at the office don’t tell us owners about this. (Updated 2018/02/28)

There’s a feeling called “a weight lifted off one’s shoulders” — that’s what it’s like after buying a house and getting the loan. Waiting for approval and finally becoming a full-fledged mortgage slave — that passage describes me. A stone in my heart finally landed.

So I asked a friend from elementary, middle, and high school to help me retrieve the loan contract from the Aijian branch and mail it to me in Beijing.

My parents didn’t contribute much to the house. It was all saved up over these years of hard drifting in Beijing after graduation, enduring the smog, saving with my life. So I don’t envy those parents who hand money to their kids to buy houses.

With that, buying the house came to a close.

If the house is registered under two names, six contracts are signed — one each for the developer, the bank, and each of the two owners.
The ownership is registered in the ratio of whoever signs 51% and whoever signs 49%.

Withdrawing Beijing’s Housing Fund

Withdrawing Beijing’s housing fund requires the following materials:

  • Original and photocopy of the purchase contract
  • Original and photocopy of the loan contract
  • Printed proof of one continuous year or more of tax payments in Beijing
  • Original purchase invoice and printed screenshot of the tax query
  • Payment receipts for the purchase
  • The company’s housing fund withdrawal form
  • Department manager’s signed approval
  • Personal signed statement affirming the authenticity of the provided materials

After all this is done, the housing fund can be withdrawn on a scheduled basis. Since Baidu, that lousy company, has poor credit, I could only withdraw once per quarter (every 3 months).

After completing the process, the Beijing Housing Fund will return a housing fund scheduled withdrawal record form to us, used as proof for others to withdraw. The next withdrawal must rely on this record form — for example, if my girlfriend wants to withdraw, she must have this record form. Remember, this is very important.

The first payment didn’t arrive until the end of June. The first thing I did was pay back all the money I’d borrowed for the house.

I just can’t figure out why my own housing fund money is so hard to withdraw. Even with all the meetings and paperwork reductions, I found that non-Beijingers are still stuck in the same old rut — only Beijingers can withdraw with ease. With a chest full of anger, I started despising that taxi driver again.

After such complicated procedures, I finally managed to buy an apartment. Thinking about it is truly bitter and helpless — sad and dispirited. Buying a house in China is just this troublesome. To those who’ll buy houses later: prepare for a protracted war, because we’re wrestling wits and guts with the Chinese system.

A Story Worth Learning from The Last Eighteen Years of the Late Qing

Fukushima Yasumasa (1852–1919), a native of Shinshu, Japan. Governor-General of the Kwantung Governor-General’s Office, Japan’s colonial organ. Army general. Known as the “father of Japanese intelligence warfare.” He was a schemer and strategist with far-sighted vision.

In 1887, Fukushima Yasumasa was appointed Japanese military attaché to Germany. During his five years in Germany, he conducted a detailed survey of the situation in European countries, and Russia’s movements drew his particular attention. Constrained by Britain’s check, Russia’s traditional southward policy was forced to slow down, and Fukushima correctly predicted that Russia would inevitably turn eastward. Sure enough, in January 1891, Russia announced the plan to build the Trans-Siberian Railway and began construction immediately. This railway — connecting the Russian capital St. Petersburg with Vladivostok (Haishenwai), crossing the Eurasian continent — could be called Russia’s greatest weapon for invading Asia. The Trans-Siberian Railway crosses northeast China; a branch extends south from Harbin straight to Lüshun and Dalian, allowing Russia to enter Asia with ease. Fukushima predicted the railway would take about ten years, and he believed those ten years would determine Japan’s survival — Japan absolutely could not stand by idly.

To learn the actual situation of Russia’s eastward policy, Fukushima decided to conduct an on-site reconnaissance along the Trans-Siberian Railway himself. This bold plan immediately received strong support from Vice Chief of General Staff Kawakami Soroku. Since Fukushima’s military identity would expose the purpose of the crossing plan, he publicly announced he would undertake an adventure of riding alone across Siberia during the bitter winter. Even native Russians wouldn’t dare casually claim they could cross Siberia in deep winter, let alone a foreigner with no experience living in freezing regions. The moment Fukushima made this announcement, the world was in an uproar. Western explorers scoffed at his “crossing” plan, and everyone waited to see this Japanese man make a fool of himself. But Fukushima Yasumasa was extremely resolute — he knew full well what this plan meant for Japan.
On February 11, 1892, Fukushima and his beloved horse “Kaisen” set off from Germany on the crossing journey, heading north in the bitter cold of minus 20 degrees Celsius. In late March, after arriving in St. Petersburg, Fukushima sent the Imperial General Headquarters his first intelligence report on the Russian army. By then, the Russian side had vaguely sensed that Fukushima’s “trip” was a case of “brandishing the sword at Xiangzhuang while aiming at the Duke of Pei” — his real intentions lay elsewhere. On April 9, Fukushima left St. Petersburg and arrived in Moscow in late April, where he was received and feasted by the Tsar and Tsarina. After careful investigation, Fukushima submitted a report on the Trans-Siberian Railway construction to the Imperial General Headquarters. In late September, Fukushima reached the border mountains between China and Russia — the Altai Mountains, over 3,000 meters above sea level. Looking far into the distance from the Altai Mountains, all he could see was white snow. At this point, Fukushima had traveled 7,000 km — half the journey done. Fukushima Yasumasa welcomed 1893 during the journey. Late January through February is the coldest period in Siberia, with temperatures reaching minus 50 degrees. Russians almost never went out in such harsh winter, but Fukushima pressed on through the uninhabited frozen wilderness with astonishing willpower. Ultimately, relying on his tenacious perseverance, Fukushima walked out of Siberia. Following the gradually warming Heilongjiang River southward into China, Fukushima Yasumasa spent another two-plus months gathering military intelligence in Aihui, Qiqihar, Jilin, and elsewhere. After more than a year of arduous trekking, Fukushima was exhausted in both body and mind. He finally reached the terminus — Vladivostok. In this crossing of Siberia, Fukushima Yasumasa changed horses at least eight times, spent 488 days, and covered 14,000 km — setting a miracle in the history of intelligence reconnaissance. The Japanese living in Vladivostok welcomed Fukushima’s arrival with wild joy. The whole world was reporting extensively on his solo crossing of Siberia. Fukushima Yasumasa became a world celebrity. Emperor Meiji specially awarded him the Order of the Rising Sun, 3rd Class, and personally hosted a banquet in his honor. The first-hand intelligence Fukushima obtained during the crossing became a crucial information guarantee for Japan’s victory in the Russo-Japanese War.

A country’s intelligence work cannot rely on the strength of a few individuals alone; only through long-term cultivation can a vast and effective intelligence network be formed. In this regard, Fukushima was also an old hand.

This Japanese man crossed the Russian Eurasian Far East railway — today’s Moscow » Manzhouli » Qiqihar » Daqing » Harbin » Mudanjiang » Vladivostok international train route for trade with Russia.

Just to gather strategic intelligence, he mapped all the mountains, terrain, landforms, and rivers along the railway (back then there was no Google satellite positioning map) — making full preparations for war.

This man’s will deeply inspired me. If a country wants to invade another, producing one or two talents like him is about all it takes.
